title: "Pea Pressure"

slug: "pea-pressure"
blurb: "Dad performs his own chaotic, pun-riddled version of The Princess and the Pea for his two daughters at bedtime — one who believes every word, and one who's almost too old to keep falling for it. Almost."
---
“Once upon a time,” Dad began, “there lived a prince who desperately wanted to marry a real princess.”

“What’s a real princess?” asked Lily, who was six and believed Dad knew nearly everything.

“A princess who exists,” said Dad. “The imaginary ones are very difficult to invite to dinner.”

Lily nodded seriously.

Beside her, nine-year-old Sophie narrowed her eyes. She had recently discovered that approximately half of what Dad said was nonsense. The problem was figuring out which half.

“The prince travelled all over the world,” Dad continued. “He searched in France, Spain, Italy, and even the kingdom behind the sofa.”

“There’s no kingdom behind the sofa,” said Sophie.

“Not anymore. It was conquered by dust bunnies.”

Lily gasped. Sophie did not. But she did glance at the sofa.

“The prince met many princesses, but there was always something wrong. One princess wore her crown backwards. Another ate peas with a knife.”

“Is that bad?” Lily asked.

“Terrible. The peas keep rolling off. Dinner takes hours.”

“And that means she wasn’t a princess?” Sophie asked.

“No, it meant she needed a spoon.”

Sophie folded her arms. “You’re changing the story.”

“I’m improving it. The original has hardly any dragons.”

“Does this one have dragons?”

“No. But now you don’t know if one will show up later. That’s dramatic tension.”

“That’s the workshop voice,” Sophie said.

“The what?” Lily asked.

“He teaches a writing class on Tuesdays. Sometimes it leaks into bedtime.”

Dad cleared his throat — which was as close as he ever came to admitting it — and carried on.

“One dark and stormy night, someone knocked on the castle gate.”

KNOCK! KNOCK! KNOCK!

“Who’s there?” Lily asked automatically.

“Interrupting princess.”

“Interrupting prin—”

“YOUR HIGHNESS!”

Lily burst out laughing. Sophie groaned into her pillow.

Dad carried on proudly.

“At the gate stood a girl soaked from head to toe. Water dripped from her hair, her dress, and—”

“Her nose?” said Sophie.

Dad looked impressed. “Exactly. She was raining from every available surface.”

“She said, ‘Please let me in. I am a true princess.’”

“Did they believe her?” Lily asked.

“The prince did. But the queen wasn’t convinced. She had met plenty of people claiming to be royalty. Just the week before, a man had arrived declaring himself the Duke of Pudding.”

“Was he?” Lily whispered.

“No. He was a custard impersonator.”

Sophie pulled the blanket over her face. Underneath it she had a whole sentence ready, the kind that would flatten the entire story in four words. She lowered the blanket enough to look at Lily, who was still leaning forward, still waiting for the pea, and put the sentence away instead.

“So the queen devised a test. She went to the guest room, removed all the blankets, and placed one tiny green pea beneath the mattress.”

Dad smiled. Sophie was becoming dangerously difficult to fool.

“Then she stacked another mattress on top. And another. And another.”

“How many?” asked Lily.

“Twenty.”

“That’s ridiculous,” Sophie said.

“They had a very good mattress sale. Buy nineteen, get one free. The bed got so tall the princess needed a ladder, climbing boots, and a small packed lunch just to reach the top.”

Lily giggled.

“And in case she rolled off in the night, the queen put a trampoline beside the bed. Royal safety regulation — the paperwork alone took three jesters.”

Sophie started to object, then sat up instead. “Wait. If the trampoline was right there the whole time, why did she need the ladder and the boots and the lunch? Couldn’t she just bounce all the way to the top?”

Dad opened his mouth. Nothing came out. He closed it again.

“...Dad?”

“I’m thinking.”

“You never used to take this long.”

“This is a very structurally important question. It deserves silence.”

“Workshop voice,” Sophie said. She waited anyway, arms folded, chin lifted.

“Fine,” Dad said. “She tried the trampoline first. Overshot it by four mattresses and landed in her own packed lunch. After that, they went with the ladder. Satisfied?”

“Barely.” But she was still smiling.

“The princess climbed all the way up. She put on her pyjamas—”

“Princess pyjamas?” Lily asked.

“Of course. They had little pictures of ordinary people on them.”

Even Sophie laughed at that.

“That night, the rain crashed against the windows. Thunder shook the castle. The wind howled. The princess tossed and turned.”

“Because of the pea?” Lily asked.

“We don’t know. Someone had accidentally given her coffee after dinner.”

“Dad!”

“All right, all right. In the morning, the queen asked, ‘How did you sleep?’”

“‘Terribly,’ said the princess. ‘Something hard was under my bed. I am black and blue all over!’”

Lily’s eyes widened. “She felt the pea through all those mattresses?”

“She did.”

“That’s impossible,” Sophie declared.

“Which proved she was a real princess.”

“Or that they needed better mattresses,” Sophie said.

Dad leaned closer and lowered his voice.

“The prince was delighted. At last, he had found a true princess. They were married, and the pea was placed in the Royal Museum, where no one was allowed to eat it, even when the cafeteria ran out of vegetables.”

“And they lived happily ever after?” Lily asked.

“Almost.”

Sophie sat up. “There’s no ‘almost.’ That’s the end.”

“Not according to the extremely old and completely trustworthy book I just made up.”

Dad continued.

“On the first night after the wedding, the prince complained that he couldn’t sleep.”

“Why?” Lily asked.

“He said there was something horribly uncomfortable beneath his pillow.”

“The princess looked under it and found…”

Dad paused.

“A chocolate biscuit.”

Lily stared. “How did that get there?”

“The princess had hidden it for a midnight snack. But the prince said, ‘I felt it through my pillow! I must be a real princess too!’”

Lily laughed so hard she fell sideways.

Sophie shook her head. “That doesn’t even make sense.”

“It makes perfect sense,” said Dad. “And from that day on, the kingdom stopped testing princesses with peas. They tested everyone with biscuits instead.”

“What happened if they felt one?” Lily asked.

“They joined the royal family.”

“And if they didn’t?”

“They ate the biscuit.”

“That’s not a bad test,” Sophie admitted.

Dad tucked the blankets around them. “And now it’s time for two princesses to go to sleep.”

“I’m not a princess,” Sophie said.

Dad pointed at her mattress. “We’ll see about that. I may have put a pea underneath it.”

Lily immediately jumped out of bed and began searching.

Sophie stayed where she was.

Dad raised an eyebrow. “Not going to check?”

“No,” said Sophie calmly. “I’m too old to fall for that.”

She pulled the blanket up to her chin anyway and waited, fighting down a smile.

“That’s my girl. Good night.”

He kissed them both on the forehead, switched off the light, and walked towards the door. At the frame, he paused, one hand on the wood. “You’re smiling.”

“I’m not,” said Sophie, and stopped.

He looked at her a second longer, then shrugged and kept going.

“Dad?” Sophie called.

“Yes?”

“There’s something under your pillow.”

Dad stopped.

Sophie’s voice was perfectly serious.

“I put it there before bedtime.”

“What?”

“A spider.”

Dad stared into the darkness.

“A real one?”

Sophie snuggled beneath her blanket. “Good night, Princess.”

Dad hurried from the room.

Lily whispered, “If there is a spider, can I keep it?”

“No,” said Sophie.

From the hallway came a long silence, then the clatter of a broom hitting the floor.

Sophie smiled.

“But he’d never grown up enough to stop falling for it.”
