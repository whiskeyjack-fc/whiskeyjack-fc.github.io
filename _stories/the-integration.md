Dear Mom,

I'm so sorry. I know I promised I'd be home this year, and I really meant it. It's been what, three years now? I had the whole trip planned out—even packed my bag before the ceremony. I was going to surprise you.

But things changed about ten minutes after the ceremony ended.

They pulled a dozen of us aside while everyone else was celebrating. At first I thought we'd screwed something up, forgotten to submit some final paperwork or something. But then we were in a private room with representatives from The Company, and they were shaking our hands and saying we'd been selected for immediate deployment. Priority assignment. No application process, no interview. Just: congratulations, you leave in four hours.

Four hours, Mom.

I should be excited, right? This is what everyone works toward. Most graduates spend months waiting for placement, taking temporary positions, hoping to catch someone's attention. And here I am, recruited on the spot. The Company must have been watching my work more closely than I realized.

I won't lie—there was a moment where I wondered if your position had something to do with it. But they showed me my research scores, my thesis evaluation. Top of my division. So no, this wasn't a favor. This was earned. I need to remember that, especially on days when the doubt creeps in.

They rushed us back to the dorms to grab our things. I was lucky. Like I said, I'd already packed for home. Just had to swap out some clothes. The others were scrambling. One girl was on the phone trying to explain to her family why she couldn't make it to dinner.

No time for goodbyes either. I tried calling you but couldn't get through. They were rushing us and I only had a few minutes. I left a message but I'm not sure if it reached you. That's the part that really stings. I wanted to see you, Mom. Tell you everything in person. Thank you properly for all those years.

I'm writing this from a hydrofoil somewhere over the Atlantic, I think. They haven't told us where we're going yet. Just that it's a research vessel, a mobile lab. I don't even know what department I'll be working in. Marine biology? Climate systems? They're being weirdly secretive about the details.

The cabin's cramped and the engine noise is constant, but there's a window at least. I can see the ocean stretching out forever. It's beautiful, in a lonely sort of way. Reminds me of all those times you brought me along on your field work. I probably spent more time on research vessels as a kid than most scientists do in their first year. Maybe that's why this feels familiar, even when it shouldn't.

Anyway, I promise I'll write as soon as I know more. And who knows, maybe this assignment will be short. Maybe I'll surprise you at New Year's instead.

Love you,

Pieter

Dear Mom,

Well, I'm here. Wherever "here" is.

I wish I had better news. I wish I could tell you this place is everything I hoped for, that the assignment makes sense, that I understand why they rushed us out here. But I'd be lying, and I've never been good at lying to you.

The research vessel is... not what I expected. You know those stations you used to take me to? The ones that hummed with purpose, where everyone seemed to know exactly what they were doing and why it mattered? This isn't like that. At all.

When we pulled up alongside it yesterday, my first thought was that we had the wrong coordinates. The ship looked abandoned. Rust streaks running down the hull, observation windows clouded with salt spray and grime. One of the loading cranes was just sitting there at a broken angle, like nobody had bothered to fix it in months. Maybe years.

I spent the last two days on the hydrofoil with three other recent graduates, all pulled from their ceremonies. Different campuses, different specializations. We didn't talk much at first—I think we were all still processing the whiplash of it all. But by the second day, we'd started comparing notes. Trying to figure out why we were chosen, where we were going, what we'd be working on.

Nobody had answers. We were all just... picked.

The welcome committee consisted of exactly one person: a research assistant who looked like he'd rather be literally anywhere else. Apparently the project lead who was supposed to greet us was "indisposed." The assistant didn't elaborate, but from his tone and the way he kept checking his watch, I got the impression this wasn't the first time. When we passed the rec room later, I could smell stale alcohol and see empty bottles through the doorway.

The tour took maybe fifteen minutes. Lab spaces, living quarters, galley, emergency protocols. All delivered in a monotone, like he was reading from a script he'd memorized years ago and resented having to repeat. Then he just... left us. Said we'd get our assignments in the morning and walked off.

The four of us ended up in the galley around dinner time, not really sure what else to do. It wasn't planned. We just sort of gravitated toward each other, the only familiar faces in a sea of strangers who didn't seem particularly interested in meeting us.

Reyna stared at her tray for a moment before saying, "You know what? I spent four years dreaming about cutting-edge research." She looked up and caught my eye. "I should've been more specific." We all actually laughed at that. It felt good.

Rin asked if anyone else thought this felt off. She didn't mean the ship itself (though yeah, obviously) but the whole situation. Why the secrecy? Why the rush? Why us? David barely said anything. He just kept looking around at the ship, at the handful of disinterested crew members, shaking his head slightly. I got the sense he was the kind of person who needed time to process before speaking.

None of us had good answers. But even without saying much, it was clear we were all thinking the same thing.

I keep telling myself this is just the adjustment period. That every new posting feels strange at first. That The Company knows what it's doing, and there's a reason we're here, and it'll all make sense once we actually start working.

I'm trying to believe that, Mom. I really am.

But right now, sitting in this cramped cabin listening to the hull creak and the endless drone of the engines—the same sounds I grew up with on your expeditions, except emptier somehow—I can't shake the feeling that something's wrong.

Maybe I'm overthinking it. Maybe I'm just tired and disappointed and adjusting to the reality of entry-level work. Tomorrow we'll get our assignments and everything will click into place.

That's what I'm telling myself anyway.

I'll write again soon.

Love you,

Pieter

Dear Mom,

It's been three weeks. Three weeks of this.

I keep waiting for things to get better, for the real work to start, for someone to explain why they pulled me out of graduation and rushed me here. But every day is more of the same.

My assignment finally came through that first morning, like they promised. I'm working under Dr. Castellano. He's... different from the others here. I barely see him, but not because he's negligent or drunk like most of the senior staff. He's always working. His lab is locked most of the time, and when I do catch him in the corridors, he's distracted, preoccupied. He'll ask me brief questions about my work (sharp questions that show he's actually paying attention) but then he's gone again before I can ask anything back.

He gave me sensor array maintenance as my primary duty. Water monitoring, salinity readings, temperature logs. It's basic work, but at least the equipment is functional and the data is actually being recorded properly. When I asked if I could help with his research directly, he just said "Not yet" and walked away. Not "no." "Not yet."

I don't know what to make of him. Everyone else on this ship seems to have given up, but Castellano? He's working on something. I can tell. The question is what, and why won't he let anyone help?

Meanwhile, the actual work environment here is... it's a mess, Mom.

Rin's supervisor, Dr. Hendricks, is everything Castellano isn't. He's the one who was supposed to meet us when we arrived but was too hungover to function, remember? Rin says he barely shows up to the lab, and when he does, he spends more time making uncomfortable comments than actually supervising. She tries to laugh it off, but it's clearly wearing on her. Last week at one of the parties, I watched him corner her by the drinks table and I almost said something. David stopped me, said it would just make things worse for her. I hate that he's probably right.

Reyna's in the biology division under someone who seems nice enough but completely checked out. She's cataloging frozen samples from projects that ended years ago. She jokes about it, calls herself the "Chief Freezer Inspector," but I can tell it's getting to her too. David's doing equipment maintenance—literally just changing filters and running diagnostics.

The four of us have fallen into a routine. We eat together, vent together, try to keep each other's spirits up. Reyna's the glue, honestly. She has this way of making even the most mind-numbing day feel bearable. Yesterday she found an old karaoke machine in storage and dragged us all to the rec room. David actually sang. It was terrible and perfect.

I'm trying not to think about how different this is from what I imagined. From what you probably imagined for me too.

The social scene here is... bizarre. There's a party almost every other night. Not small gatherings—actual parties with music loud enough to rattle the bulkheads, enough alcohol to float the ship twice over, and substances I'm not naive enough to ignore. At first I stayed away, but Reyna pointed out that refusing to go just makes you an outsider. And the few people who stay isolated here? They look miserable.

So I go. I nurse one drink, talk to Reyna and the others, try to understand what everyone is running from. Because that's what it feels like—everyone here is trying very hard not to think about something. The work is just an excuse. The real goal seems to be numbing themselves enough to forget why they ended up here.

Castellano never comes to these parties. I've never seen him drink, never seen him in the rec room. He's always in his lab or in his quarters. A few of the older researchers make jokes about him, call him "the monk," but there's something else in their voices when they talk about him. Resentment, maybe? Or jealousy that he still cares about something?

There's another party this weekend. Someone's birthday, I think. Reyna's already threatened to drag me there physically if I try to skip it. Part of me wants to stay in my cabin and read, but I know she's right—isolation isn't the answer.

There's a researcher, Dr. Voss, who sometimes sits with us at meals. She's been here eight years. Eight years. I made the mistake of asking what she was working on and she just stared at me like I'd asked something profound and sad. Then she smiled and said, "Staying employed." That was it.

Reyna says I need to give it time. "You've been here three weeks. Maybe it gets better." She's trying to stay optimistic, and I appreciate that. But I've seen Dr. Voss. I've seen what eight years looks like.

I know I should be grateful. I have a job, a salary, housing, food. Plenty of my classmates are still waiting for placements. But it feels like I'm wasting time, Mom. Every day I spend maintaining sensors someone else could monitor, I'm falling behind. My skills are getting dull. My thesis research is collecting digital dust.

Is this what The Company does with people it doesn't know what to do with? Just... park them somewhere and forget about them? But then why is Castellano here? He doesn't fit the pattern.

Unless... unless Castellano meant what he said. "Not yet." Like there might be something more if I'm patient.

Maybe I just need to adjust my expectations. Maybe this is what entry-level work really looks like, and campus just didn't prepare us for that reality.

But I keep thinking about your field stations. The purpose. The energy. People worked because they believed in what they were doing. That's what I wanted. That's what I thought I'd find.

Castellano has that energy. It shows in the way he moves, the way he talks. He's working toward something. I just need to figure out how to prove I could help him with it.

Miss you,

Pieter

Dear Mom,

Something changed.

I almost can't believe I'm writing this, but Castellano asked me to help with his research. Actually asked me. After weeks of "not yet" and locked doors and wondering if I was wasting my time here, he finally let me in.

It happened last night. I was finishing my shift, running the usual diagnostics on the sensor arrays, when he appeared in the lab. Not rushing through like usual, but actually there, waiting for me. He asked if I had plans for the evening. I almost laughed—plans? On this ship? But I just said no.

"Good," he said. "Come with me."

That was it. No explanation, no context. Just followed him through corridors I'd never been down before, past labs with darkened windows and locked doors, until we reached his workspace. It's tucked away in the aft section, far from the main research areas. Isolated. Private.

The lab itself is nothing like the rest of the ship. Everything is clean, organized, functional. Modern equipment, proper lighting, actual active experiments running. It felt like stepping back onto campus. Like finding out there was a completely different ship hidden inside this one.

He didn't waste time on pleasantries. "You've been maintaining the offshore sensor arrays," he said. "Have you noticed anything unusual in the water composition data?"

I had, actually. Small variations in the organic particulate readings, patterns that didn't match seasonal norms. I'd flagged them in my reports but assumed no one was reading them.

"I read them," Castellano said, like he could hear what I was thinking. "You're observant. Good. That's why you're here."

He showed me what he's been working on. It's a strain of algae—or something like algae. He was careful about how he described it. Found it three years ago in deep water samples from a trench system we're not supposed to be near. The Company has restrictions on certain zones, territorial disputes with the African Federation, but Castellano doesn't seem concerned about that.

The algae is remarkable, Mom. It has these symbiotic properties I've never seen before. When exposed to organic tissue (he demonstrated with fish samples) it integrates itself into the cellular structure. Not parasitically, but cooperatively. It enhances the host. Faster muscle response, increased oxygen efficiency, accelerated healing. The implications for medical research alone are staggering.

This is real science. The kind of work I thought I'd be doing here from the start. The kind of work that matters.

I spent four hours in that lab with him, just learning, observing, asking questions. He answered most of them, though some he deflected. There are things he's still not telling me, I can feel that. But he's letting me see enough to know this is important. Maybe dangerous, given the secrecy. But important.

When I finally left, it was past midnight. The corridors were empty except for the distant sound of another party somewhere in the rec section. I didn't care. For the first time since I arrived, I felt like I had a purpose here.

Castellano told me to come back the next evening. Same time. He wants me to help with the next phase of trials. He didn't say what that entails, but I don't care. This is what I've been waiting for.

I wanted to tell Reyna and the others, but something stopped me. Castellano didn't explicitly say to keep quiet, but the way he works (the isolation, the locked doors, the late hours) makes it clear this isn't public knowledge. I don't want to do anything that might jeopardize this opportunity.

Is that wrong? To keep this from them? They're my friends, and we've been honest with each other about everything. But this feels different. This feels like it needs to stay private, at least for now.

Maybe once I understand more about what we're doing, I can explain it to them properly.

I know you always taught me to be careful about who I trust and what I share in professional settings. The Company has eyes everywhere, you used to say. I'm trying to be smart about this. Castellano trusts me enough to bring me in. I don't want to betray that trust.

But I also can't help feeling excited, Mom. Finally, after all these weeks of meaningless work and watching everyone else give up, I've found something worth doing. Something that could actually make a difference.

The algae samples glow faintly in their containment units. Bioluminescent, Castellano says, though he doesn't know why yet. There's so much we don't understand about them. So much potential.

I can't wait to get back in that lab.

Talk soon,

Pieter

I woke up and there was a patch on my palm. Small, maybe the size of a coin. The skin there looked different. Darker, with a faint shimmer to it, almost iridescent. Like oil on water, but part of me. It didn't hurt. If anything, it felt good. Stronger somehow, even though that doesn't make sense.

I went straight to Castellano. He examined it for a long time without speaking, then he did something I didn't expect. He smiled.

"Don't panic," he said. "This is actually remarkable. You're integrating."

He explained that the algae doesn't just attach to tissue, it becomes part of it. Rewriting cellular structure at a fundamental level. Most of his test subjects rejected the integration or showed violent immune responses. But my body accepted it. The patch on my palm was the algae establishing itself, creating what he called a "symbiotic matrix."

He asked if he could study it. Document the progression. "This could be important," he said. "Revolutionary, even."

I said yes. What else could I say? It's already happening. I might as well understand it.

That was two days ago. The patch has grown since then. It covers most of my palm now and has started spreading up my fingers and toward my wrist. The texture is strange, not quite skin but not foreign either. It's become part of me in a way that feels more natural than it should.

And the strength. Mom, you wouldn't believe it. My grip strength has at least doubled. I tested it on Castellano's equipment yesterday. The affected hand can crush things the other can't even hold properly. It's exhilarating and terrifying at the same time.

Castellano says the algae is enhancing the muscle tissue all the way up to my forearm, improving oxygen efficiency and fiber strength at the cellular level. The patch itself is forming something like an exoskeleton, a protective layer that's surprisingly flexible but incredibly strong. Everything I'm experiencing matches his previous observations with test subjects, except I'm conscious and cooperative. I can report what it feels like from the inside.

He's excited. More animated than I've ever seen him. This is what he's been working toward for three years. A viable human integration.

But there are side effects. He warned me about them yesterday.

The aggression is subtle at first. Little things. Impatience during conversations. A shorter temper when equipment doesn't work right. Yesterday someone bumped into me in the corridor and I spun around ready to say something harsh, something mean, before I caught myself. The look on their face made me realize how I must have looked in that moment.

It scared me.

I asked Castellano if it would get worse. He hesitated, then admitted that in previous subjects, the behavioral changes escalated as the integration spread. Increased aggression, erratic movement patterns, attacking other subjects in their tanks. But those were fish, smaller mammals. He doesn't know exactly how it will manifest in humans because I'm the first human case that's progressed this far.

I'm the experiment now.

I think he might be right.

I'll write again soon,

## Chapter 8: Breaking Point

The storm hit hardest around midnight.

Reyna sat in the galley with fourteen other crew members, waiting it out. The ship rolled with each swell, groaning under the strain. Emergency lighting cast everything in dim red. Some people clustered around tables, talking in low voices. Others sat alone, staring at phones with no signal. Dr. Voss stood by the viewport, watching waves crash against the glass, arms crossed. Eight years on this ship. She'd weathered worse storms.

Someone's coffee mug slid across the table and clattered to the floor. Nobody bothered picking it up.

David sat across from Reyna, chewing the same protein bar he'd been working on for twenty minutes. Not hungry, just needing something to do with his hands. His eyes kept flicking to the door. To the empty seats.

Reyna felt it too. The wrongness.

Pieter hadn't left his quarters in three days.

Rin hadn't been seen since dinner.

"Where's Rin?" Reyna said quietly.

David's eyes flicked up. "Bathroom maybe?"

"For an hour?"

He stopped chewing. Set the protein bar down on the table.

The ship pitched hard to port. Someone cursed. A chair slid across the floor and crashed into a table. When Reyna looked around, she realized Dr. Hendricks wasn't there either. Had been gone for a while. She hadn't noticed him leave.

Something cold settled in her stomach.

"David."

He was already standing.

They moved through the corridor together, bracing against the walls with each violent roll. The storm screamed outside, metal groaning under the pressure of waves hitting the hull.

"Rin?" David called.

No answer.

They checked the bathroom first. Empty. Her quarters. Empty. David's hand hovered over Pieter's door, then pulled back.

"Not yet," he muttered.

They kept moving. Down to the lab levels. Storage sections. Following instinct more than logic. The storm covered everything in noise, waves crashing against the hull, wind howling through vents.

Then they heard it. Faint. A voice saying "No" over and over.

"David."

He heard it too. They ran.

The sounds grew louder as they got closer. Heavy breathing. Fabric tearing. A voice \- Hendricks, drunk-slurred \- saying things that made Reyna's throat close. Then that other voice again, smaller, terrified.

Reyna caught movement in the side corridor. A shadow. But the ship rolled hard and she lost sight of it.

David hit the door at a run.

The storage room was barely ten feet across. Crates stacked against the walls. Hendricks had Rin pinned in the corner, one hand over her mouth, the other tearing at her clothes. Rin's eyes were wide, bleeding from a split lip.

"Get off her\!" David grabbed Hendricks by the shoulder.

Hendricks spun, faster than a drunk man should move, and backhanded David across the face. David went down hard, head slamming into a crate.

Reyna froze. She should move. Should scream. Should do something.

Movement from the doorway. A scraping sound. Metal on metal. Then breathing. Heavy. Wrong.

Hendricks heard it too. He turned, squinting into the shadows. "Who's—"

No.

Not Pieter anymore.

Where skin met exoskeleton, the boundary pulsed with faint bioluminescence.

But his face. That was worse. Half-human, half something else. The right side of his jaw looked reinforced, bone structure shifted. His eye on that side had no white anymore, just black iris and a pupil that reflected the red emergency lighting like an animal's.

He looked at Rin. At the blood on her face. At Hendricks' hand still gripping her arm.

"Let her go."

His voice came out layered. Human words underneath something that vibrated in the base register, felt in the chest more than heard.

Pieter moved.

Reyna's brain couldn't process the speed. One moment he stood in the doorway. The next his armored hand was around Hendricks' throat, lifting him off the ground. Hendricks' eyes bulged. His hands clawed at Pieter's wrist.

The armor didn't even dent.

"Pieter," Reyna whispered.

Then he looked back at Hendricks.

"Please," Hendricks choked out. "I'm sorry. I—"

The sound of Hendricks' throat collapsing cut off the words. Pieter's hand tightened. Cartilage crunched. Blood vessels burst. Hendricks thrashed, feet kicking empty air.

And pulled.
