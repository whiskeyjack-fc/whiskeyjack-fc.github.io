The house smells like someone else's laundry detergent. Three days in and I still notice it — that particular sweetness that isn't mine, clinging to the towels, the couch cushions, the blanket I pull over myself at night because I can't find the thermostat.

The dog's name is Beau. He's a mid-sized mutt with one ear that stands up and one that doesn't, and he has decided, after seventy-two hours of cautious assessment, that I am acceptable. Not loved. Acceptable. He follows me from room to room but keeps a precise distance. Close enough to monitor, far enough to bolt if I turn out to be wrong.

I understand the instinct.

The town is the same and not the same. The bakery on Herschel Street closed. Something called "Nourish" is there now, all clean lines and matcha. The video store, which was already an anachronism when I left, is just gone. Vacant lot. But the harbour still smells like diesel and salt and seaweed drying on the boat ramp — that low-tide rot, warm and thick, coating the back of your throat when you breathe through your mouth. I walked past it yesterday morning and stopped without deciding to. Stood on the footpath with my eyes closed while a bus went past behind me and the smell came off the water in slow pulses. Not praying. Just — the smell had arrived somewhere in my chest before my brain caught up to it. Or the smell was remembering me.

I'm here because Lena asked. Lena who I worked with for two years at the community centre, who moved into this house after I moved out of the town, who needed someone for two weeks while she visited her mother in Christchurch. She asked three people before me. I know this because she told me, which is the kind of honesty I've always liked about Lena and the exact kind that makes you feel like a fourth choice, which you are.

It's fine. I needed somewhere to be.

---

On the third evening, I feed Beau at six. The exact time written on the instruction sheet stuck to the fridge, along with the vet's number, the neighbour's number, and a hand-drawn map to the nearest off-leash park. I measure the kibble with the specific cup Lena left out, add the quarter-tin of wet food, and set it on the mat by the back door. Beau eats like it might be taken from him. I leave him to it.

I go out to the small back garden to sit on the step while he finishes. The evening is doing that thing it does here in late autumn. Not getting dark so much as thickening. The air has weight. I can smell rain coming, or the ocean. Sometimes they're the same thing.

The gate latch lifts.

Small sound, metal on metal. And then the scrabble of nails on concrete, and by the time I'm on my feet Beau is a brown shape rounding the corner of the house and disappearing down the driveway.

"Beau. Beau!"

Nothing.

I grab my jacket from the hook by the back door and my phone from the kitchen counter and I'm running after him in shoes I haven't tied, and the instruction sheet on the fridge has a line about this: *If he gets out, don't chase. Walk calmly. He'll come back to food sounds.* He just ate. And I am absolutely chasing him.

He's fast. Faster than anything with that build should be. He stays on the footpath like he has a destination. He knows this town better than I do. I follow him past the primary school, past the dairy that still has the same faded Tip Top sign, and he doesn't slow down and I don't catch up and my untied laces are about to settle this for me.

Three blocks. Four. He cuts left onto the path that runs behind the community centre, and I know where he's going before I see it. The off-leash park. The one on Lena's fridge map. He's been here before. He knows the way.

I stop running. Partly because I trust that he's going somewhere contained. Partly because my lungs have made a decision without consulting me. I walk the last stretch with my hands on my hips, breathing like someone who used to run and doesn't anymore, and when I come through the gap in the hedge the park opens up. A rough oval of grass bordered by pohutukawa trees, a single bench, a water fountain that has never worked. And Beau is there, standing in the middle of the field with his nose down, tail moving slowly. Unbothered.

There's someone on the bench.

I see her before I place her. My chest does something. Not dramatic. A shift, like adjusting your weight on a boat.

Sam.

She's sitting with one leg drawn up, a book open on her knee, and her eyes are on Beau instead of the page. She hasn't seen me yet. Or she has and she's giving me the option.

Sam worked at the community centre too. Not with me. Adjacent. She ran the youth programme in the room next to mine and we shared a kitchen the size of a cupboard and I knew her the way you know someone you see every day and never quite reach. We had conversations that were always in the middle. Interrupted by the kettle, by someone else arriving, by the fact that one of us was always leaving.

I never found out what would happen if we got to the end of one.

"Hey," I say, and it comes out rough from the running.

She looks up. Takes a second, then places me.

"Hey." She looks down. "Hey, Beau." Back to me. "Where's Lena?"

"Christchurch. I'm watching him."

"Right."

Beau has found whatever scent he was tracking. He circles it in that methodical way dogs have.

I should get him and go. That would be the natural thing. Clip the lead on, walk home, secure the gate, text Lena something light about her escapist dog. I have the lead in my jacket pocket. I can feel it there.

I sit down on the other end of the bench.

The wood is damp. I feel it through my jeans immediately and I don't move. Sam doesn't say anything about me sitting down. The park is almost dark now. The pohutukawa branches make shapes against the last strip of sky that isn't cloud, and somewhere behind us a tui is doing that thing where it sounds like it's arguing with itself.

"I didn't know you were back," she says. Not accusatory. Factual.

"I'm not. I'm just here for two weeks. The house-sitting."

"Right."

"You're still here," I say, and then hear it. "I mean. Sorry. That sounded like—"

"No, it's a fair question." She closes the book over her thumb to keep the page. "I keep meaning to leave. Something always comes up."

"Good somethings or bad somethings?"

She thinks about this. Actually thinks, not just pauses before an answer she already has. I'd forgotten she does that. Takes the question seriously even when it's half a joke.

"Both," she says. "Mostly just things."

Beau trots over and sits at her feet, not mine. Traitor. She reaches down and scratches behind his up-ear without looking, the way people do when touch is habitual, not calculated.

"He's particular," she says.

"He tolerates me."

"High praise from a dog."

We don't say anything for a moment. I'm aware of every word I don't choose.

The tui goes quiet. The rain starts. Not dramatically. A thickening of the air that was already thick, and then a softness on my face that could be mist or could be drizzle, and then it's rain, undeniably. Beau doesn't move. Sam doesn't move. She closes the book, keeps it in her lap. I can see the cover from here — something I don't recognise, a library sticker on the spine.

"It's not very good," she says.

"The book?"

"Mm. I keep reading it anyway. I think I'm hoping it becomes good. Which it won't."

"That's very loyal."

"That's very me."

She says this without weight, without looking at me, and it lands anyway. The rain is on my neck now, finding the gap between my collar and my skin, and I should suggest we go somewhere. The overhang by the community centre, the dairy, anywhere with a roof. But I don't want to be the one who ends this. Whatever this is. This sitting in the rain with a borrowed dog between us. Three years collapsed to the width of a bench.

"Why did you leave?" she asks.

I could start anywhere. I left because the job ended. I left because staying felt like settling and I was twenty-seven and terrified of settling. I left because the town was small enough that you became who people expected you to be, and I didn't know who that was yet. I left because I thought I'd come back when I was ready.

"I don't know," I say. Which is also true.

She nods.

"I used to wonder," she says, and stops. Beau shifts, puts his chin on her shoe. The rain is steady now.

"Wonder what?"

She turns the book over in her hands. "I used to wonder if we would have been friends. If you'd stayed. Actual friends. Not just—"

"Kitchen friends."

"Yeah."

The phrase sits between us. I hadn't planned it. Five-minute conversations between the kettle and the door. Half-sentences. Borrowed mugs.

Except here we are. On this bench, in the rain, three years later. Thinner. But here.

"I think we would have been," I say.

She doesn't answer right away. The rain does what rain does. Beau sighs, the full-body exhale of a dog who has found his spot and has no plans. My jeans are soaked through. The cold has settled into my knees, my thighs. I'm not moving.

Sam reaches down and adjusts Beau's ear, the floppy one, so the rain isn't pooling in it.

"I should get him home," I say. My voice comes out quieter than I expected.

"Yeah." She picks up her book, holds it against her chest. "Lena's gate latch sticks. You have to lift and push at the same time."

"You've been to the house?"

"I feed Beau sometimes. When Lena works late." She pauses. "I can show you. The latch thing."

I look at her. She's looking at Beau, her hand still on his ear, rain on her face, not wiping it away. The offer is simple. Walk back with me, show me the gate. Five minutes. Maybe less. She's still not looking at me.

"Yeah," I say. "Okay."

We stand. Beau stands, shakes himself with total commitment, sprays us both. Sam laughs, shielding the book, and her shoulder touches mine for a second, and then we're walking, the three of us, through the gap in the hedge, onto the path, into the rain that has decided to stay.

I don't clip the lead on. Beau walks between us, close, choosing this.
