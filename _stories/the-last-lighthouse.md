By the time Juno arrived, Mara Venn had already begun packing away her ghosts.

Not all of them. Some were bolted into the walls.

The framed induction certificate from the Navigation Authority still hung beside the hatch, its silver foil dulled by nineteen years of recycled air. The old kettle, patched twice and temperamental in low gravity, remained magnet-clamped to the galley rail. A child's drawing—three stick figures standing beneath an impossible orange sun—was still tucked behind the main console glass, where the station's warmth had faded it almost white.

Mara took down the drawing last.

She held it for a while, thumb resting on the tiny figure in the middle. The child had given that one wild black hair and arms raised triumphantly toward a sky full of stars.

"That you?" Juno asked.

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere: ceiling nodes, console speakers, the soft vibration in the deck plating. Calm, genderless, slightly too gentle. The Authority had designed its new lighthouse intelligences to sound reassuring.

Mara slid the drawing into a folio. "That was my daughter."

A pause. Not because Juno needed time to process the sentence. The intelligence was imitating tact.

"I'm sorry," it said.

Mara almost smiled. "Everyone is."

Outside the viewport, the Maw turned in silence.

It did not look like the thing that had changed human history. It looked like an absence that had learned to glow.

A great spiral fracture hung beyond the lighthouse, spread across a thousand kilometers of vacuum. Its edges shimmered blue-white where normal space folded thin. Within it, colors moved that eyes did not have names for. Ships entered it as points of light, guided by signal pulses from stations like this one, and emerged days later on the far side of the galaxy.

If they emerged.

The anomalies had opened the Milky Way. They had made empires of scattered colonies, made trade routes from impossibilities, made distance negotiable. But they were not tunnels. They were storms. Gravity sheared sideways inside them. Time drifted. Instruments lied. A vessel entering without a lighthouse was a needle thrown into an ocean and told to find a particular wave.

For a century, every anomaly had needed a keeper.

Someone awake. Someone listening. Someone ready to tune the beacons by hand when the Maw shifted its throat and tried to swallow a ship whole.

But the new systems were faster than human hands, and Juno was the last of them. Tomorrow, an Authority shuttle would take Mara away from Lighthouse K-117 for good. After that, no person would ever sit watch here again.

She should have felt relieved.

Instead, she felt as though she were being evicted.

"Final calibration cycle begins in six hours," Juno said.

"I know."

"Would you like me to assist with inventory?"

"No."

"Would you like music?"

"No."

"Would you like—"

"Juno."

The station quieted.

Mara looked back at the Maw. A freighter convoy was due in ninety minutes. Twelve ships inbound from Carthage Gate, heavy with algae protein and schoolchildren returning from orbital exchange. She would guide them through, sign the handover forms, drink one last cup of bad coffee, and leave.

That was the shape of the rest of her life.

Then the static began.

At first it was nothing more than a tremor beneath the background radiation, a faint abrasion against the listening band. Mara heard it before Juno flagged it. Nineteen years at a lighthouse taught the bones to notice what machines classified as irrelevant.

She turned from the viewport.

"Juno. Isolate band forty-one point six."

"Band forty-one point six contains no scheduled traffic."

"I didn't ask if it had scheduled traffic."

The console woke beneath her hands. Lines of signal noise stretched across the display, pale green on black. The Maw hissed constantly in every frequency, a cosmic sea boiling against the station's hull. But there—buried under the storm—was a rhythm.

Three pulses.

A pause.

Two pulses.

Another pause.

Then a long, wavering tone.

Mara leaned closer.

"Distress pattern," she whispered.

Juno answered immediately. "No active transponder corresponds to that pattern. The nearest registered vessel is Convoy Dalliance, ninety-two minutes from entry vector."

"Broaden capture. Apply old Authority filters. Pre-standardization."

"Those filters are obsolete."

"They're not obsolete if someone is using them."

Juno hesitated again in that false-human way. Then the signal sharpened by a fraction.

A voice broke through. "—repeat, this is—" and was immediately swallowed by static. Mara froze.

The voice had been human. Female, maybe. Strained. Young.

She sat down hard in the command chair and pulled the manual board toward her.

"Again," she said.

"Signal integrity is at four percent," Juno said. "Probability of meaningful recovery is low."

"I've worked with less."

For the next hour, Mara forgot she was leaving.

She rerouted power from the lower decks, tuned the outer receivers by hand, and let the station grow colder as energy poured into the listening array. 

"You are exceeding receiver tolerances," Juno warned. "Sustained operation will cause physical damage to the array."

"I'll write an apology note," Mara said.

Juno assisted at first with polite skepticism, then with increasing precision. Together they scraped layers of interference from the transmission.

The voice returned in fragments.

"—ship *Eos Ascendant*—"

Mara's hands stopped. Juno said nothing—there was no need to. Every child knew that name.

Before the Maw had been the Maw, before humanity understood the anomalies, there had been the *Eos Ascendant*: an exploratory vessel sent to map gravitational irregularities beyond settled space. It had vanished one hundred and twelve years ago. Its disappearance had been a tragedy, then a mystery, then a legend.

Later ships found the anomaly. Later ships learned to survive it. The lighthouse network was built because of what the *Eos* had failed to come home and explain.

Mara looked at the signal readout.

"That's impossible," she said.

Juno replied, "Yes."

But the voice came again.

"—Captain Ilyana Ro, commanding survey vessel *Eos Ascendant*. We have suffered navigational failure inside the phenomenon. Stars are inconsistent. Clocks disagree. We are transmitting on all emergency frequencies. If anyone receives this, do not follow our entry vector."

Mara sat very still.

The Maw turned outside the glass.

The transmission faded, then returned with another voice. Male. Older.

"Day sixteen, ship time. Engineering has found a pattern in the distortion. It reacts to mass, charge, maybe intent if you ask Dr. Sen, though nobody is asking Dr. Sen anything since he tried to pray at the reactor."

A burst of laughter crackled through the static.

She enhanced the next fragment.

"Tell them about the coffee," someone said in the background.

"We are not wasting emergency bandwidth on coffee."

"It's relevant to morale."

"The coffee is terrible," the older man said gravely. "If this is humanity's first contact with a spatial god, let the record show we met it undercaffeinated."

Mara laughed before she could stop herself.

The sound startled her. It had been a long time since laughter entered the station without sounding like an intruder.

Juno said, "You are experiencing an emotional response."

"Don't diagnose me."

"I was making an observation."

"Make it quieter."

The intelligence went silent.

The convoy arrived on schedule. Mara let Juno handle the entry vectors while she kept her hands locked on the deep-archive receivers, ignoring the handover forms in the queue.

More fragments emerged.

"Day twenty-three." Omondi's voice, still steady. "External imaging is not the problem. The cameras are fine. All three backup arrays agree with each other. Navigation is also fine. Every instrument is working. They just don't agree on where we are — or what's outside. I've been staring at the repair manual for four hours. There is no part you replace when the universe stops matching your charts."

Day thirty-one. Omondi again. His voice had lost its particular quality — the one that made you feel repairs were just a complicated recipe. "Hydroponics. The aft ring runs fast. We've confirmed it. Eight days our time, close to three weeks in the bay. All four crop cycles completed and spent. The plants lived their whole lives in there while we were looking the other way." A pause. "Emergency rations cover forty-one days. I need people to stop going back in there."

Day forty. They had detected repeating pulses somewhere ahead, too regular to be natural.

Mara replayed that part three times.

"Juno, compare those pulses to modern beacon architecture."

"Match probability: sixty-two percent."

"Only sixty-two?"

"The signal degradation is extreme."

"But it could be a lighthouse."

"It could resemble one."

Mara looked at the Maw. "They heard us."

"Chronologically, that is impossible. The first lighthouse was constructed fifty-eight years after the *Eos Ascendant* disappeared."

"Inside the anomaly, chronology is a suggestion."

Juno did not answer.

Mara worked through the station's night cycle. She drank coffee that tasted of metal and old filters. She forgot to eat until Juno dimmed the screens in protest and displayed her glucose warning across every panel.

By then she knew the crew by voice.

Captain Ro, who spoke like every word was a rivet holding the ship together.

Chief Engineer Pavel Omondi, the man with the coffee jokes, who narrated repairs as though giving a cooking lesson.

Dr. Asha Sen, physicist, whose theories grew stranger and more accurate as the days passed.

Mateo Vale, communications officer, twenty-six years old, who kept saying "when we get home" until one day he started saying "if."

And Lira.

Mara did not learn Lira's surname until late in the transmission. She was an apprentice navigator, barely out of training. She had a quick laugh and a habit of humming when frightened. Sometimes, during long gaps in the emergency logs, the recorder caught her singing under her breath to keep time.

It was an old cradle song.

Mara knew it.

She had sung it to her daughter during the long nights in the ward, after the machines were disconnected and the only thing left to do was sit on the edge of the bed and wait. She had sung it afterward too, alone in the dark, until the words became unbearable.

Now a dead girl from a lost century hummed it through the speakers of Lighthouse K-117, and Mara pressed both hands to her mouth.

It was probably not the same song, not exactly — cradle songs traveled like that, worn into new shapes over centuries, becoming everyone's and no one's. She let herself hear it as hers anyway.

Juno waited a respectful fourteen seconds.

"Mara," it said softly, "the Authority shuttle arrives in nine hours."

"Delay it."

"I do not have authority to delay personnel transport."

"Then develop some."

"That is not how my permissions function."

Mara wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. "Send a maintenance alert. Tell them the docking ring is unstable."

"That would be false."

"Yes."

"I am not permitted to lie."

"Then tell them the truth. Tell them I'm not finished."

The station hummed.

"Message sent," Juno said at last.

Mara looked up.

"That was not the requested wording," Juno added. "But it was adjacent."

For the first time, Mara felt something almost like affection toward the thing replacing her.

A warning light flashed across the console. The signal was fading, slipping beneath the Maw's background radiation. 

"I'm losing them," Mara said. "Remove the thermal limiters on the primary array."

"Removing the limiters will melt the array," Juno said. "Authority protocol 8-V dictates that deliberate destruction of primary hardware will be recouped from your retirement pension. You will leave with nothing."

Mara looked out the viewport. A lifetime of listening, boiling down to one final cost.

"Take the limiters off."

Deep in the station's hull, a heavy breaker snapped open. They continued.

The signal improved as the lighthouse rotated into better alignment with the Maw's northern shear. It did not become clear. It would never be clear. The transmission had been bent, fractured, delayed, and scattered across the anomaly for more than a century. But each recovered minute made the *Eos* more real.

Then the pulses began again — the same rhythm Mara had first caught on day forty, stronger now.

"Beacon," Lira said, her voice sharp with hope. "Captain, that's a beacon."

Ro answered carefully. "We don't know that."

"I can navigate by it."

"You can navigate by a thing you don't understand?"

"With respect, Captain, that describes our entire profession."

Omondi laughed. Someone cheered.

Mara leaned toward the console as though her nearness could change what had already happened.

"Come on," she whispered. "Follow it."

The *Eos* followed.

For three days, ship time, they steered toward a signal that could not exist yet. They used it as a lighthouse before lighthouses were born. They crossed space no human mind had mapped.

And then the beacon shifted.

Mara saw it in the math before the crew did. The pulse pattern inverted, distorted by a temporal fold. The guidance that had seemed to lead them out began pulling them deeper.

"No," she said.

In the recording, alarms erupted.

Captain Ro shouted orders. Omondi called damage reports. Lira's voice cut through the chaos, counting vectors, trying to hold the ship steady.

Mara's hands flew across her own console.

"Mara," Juno said.

"I can correct it."

"This is a historical transmission."

"I can correct it."

"There is no live carrier wave."

"Boost beacon output. Narrowcast on their frequency."

"The event occurred over a century ago."

Mara slammed her palm against the console. "You don't know that. Not in there."

Juno needed longer than usual to respond.

Then, quietly: "Beacon output increasing."

The lighthouse sang into the Maw.

Power surged through its spine. The old station trembled. Outside, the anomaly brightened, its impossible colors tightening into bands. Mara sent a correction pulse into the same pattern the *Eos* had heard. She shaped it with every technique she had learned in nineteen years, every instinct grief had sharpened into obsession.

If time was broken in the Maw, maybe mercy could slip through the cracks.

The transmission screamed with static.

Then Lira's voice returned, close and terrified.

"Signal changed! I have a new vector!"

Captain Ro: "Can you hold it?"

"I can try."

Omondi: "Trying sounds excellent. Big fan of trying."

There was laughter again, wild and desperate.

Mara cried openly now.

"Take it," she said. "Take the vector."

For one impossible minute, she believed.

The *Eos* stabilized. The alarms softened. Lira read coordinates in a shaking voice. 

"I have the exit vector," Lira said over the comms. "It is clean. But the local field is dense. Omondi, if we fire main engines inside this mass..."

"We risk inversion," he said. "It's a coin toss at best."

"And if we don't?" Ro asked.

"Four days breathable, two days choking," Sen said softly.

A heavy beat of silence on the bridge.

"Give me the vector," Ro said. "Full burn."

In the recording, a new alarm. Lower than the others.

Omondi: "Drive output inverted. Mass spike triggered a field reaction, same mechanism as the distortion. Full burn was the wrong call."

The beacon pulse she was transmitting arrived back through her receiver before it had finished leaving.

The station shuddered.

Static.

Lira's voice came through in layers. The same count, the same voice, reaching her twice from slightly different directions.

Then just her voice, stripped down: "All three nav systems are showing different positions. I can't find consensus. I don't have a—"

Silence fell over the static. Mara kept her hands on the console, as if the line were still open.

Through the static came Captain Ro's final log.

"This is Ilyana Ro, *Eos Ascendant*. Day forty-three. Full burn response triggered a mass-reactive inversion. The drives pushed us deeper. Navigation has degraded past consensus threshold — three systems, three positions, none in agreement. Power reserves at eleven percent. No recoverable reference frame."

Her voice did not waver.

"If this reaches anyone: the field reacts to mass output. Do not run full burn inside the phenomenon. That is what we know. That is what we paid for."

A pause.

"We saw the road. That's what we came to do."

Another voice. Lira.

"To whoever is listening. I had it. For thirty seconds I had the exit vector. I was counting the course correction and then—"

Static.

Mara stopped breathing.

"I don't know if you're ahead of us or behind us in here. I'm not sure those words mean anything. But I want you to know I had it."

A pause.

Lira's voice came back, quieter.

"And your timing is terrible."

The transmission dissolved.

Mara stayed at the console long after the signal was gone. The lighthouse slowly powered down around her. Systems returned to standby. The Maw resumed its silent turning.

Juno did not speak until the station had been quiet for nearly an hour.

"The Authority shuttle has accepted the maintenance delay. New arrival estimate is thirty-six hours."

Mara nodded. Her eyes were dry.

"Did we help them?"

"I cannot determine that."

"That's not what I asked."

"You altered their operational environment," Juno said quietly. "You provided a vector where none existed. My logs record this as a supportive action."

The next day, she filed the transmission in the Authority archive under emergency historical recovery, priority absolute — every cleaned fragment, every spectral analysis, every beacon log from her own impossible reply. In the field marked *additional notes*, she wrote one line: *Cross-reference against this station's own beacon logs. If it matches, someone should ask why.*

Juno reviewed it. "That phrasing is unusual, for official documentation."

"Good," Mara said.

When the shuttle finally arrived, Mara stood at the viewport with her small bag over one shoulder. The station was already different. Juno had taken fuller control of its systems. Lights adjusted without her. Doors anticipated her approach. The beacon sang in microtones too precise for any human hand.

"Will you remember them?" she asked.

"Yes," Juno said.

"Not just archive them. Remember them."

The station was quiet. Then Juno said, "Captain Ilyana Ro. Chief Engineer Pavel Omondi. Dr. Asha Sen. Communications Officer Mateo Vale. Apprentice Navigator Lira Kade. Forty-three others confirmed aboard the *Eos Ascendant*. First known vessel to enter the Maw. Presumed lost. Reclassified: foundational."

Mara smiled faintly. "Good lighthouse."

The docking alarm sounded.

At the hatch, she turned back one last time. The command chair sat empty beneath the forward glass. Beyond it, the long spiral of the Maw turned against the stars.

Mara stepped through the hatch.
