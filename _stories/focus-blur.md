The butterflies don't hold still.

I've been crouching at the edge of the flower bed for five minutes, moving in slow increments, holding my breath. Every time I press the shutter I know before I look — the preview screen confirms it. Orange smear on blue-green blur. Nothing.

"Can I?"

Maya is beside me. I didn't hear her cross the grass. She holds out her hand for the camera and I give it to her without thinking. She doesn't look at the screen. She looks at the lens. Specifically at the barrel, at a small toggle near the base. She reads it, clicks it with her thumbnail.

"AF/MF. It was set to manual." She hands it back. "Your lens wasn't autofocusing."

I look at the switch. Small, clearly labelled. I've owned this camera for two years.

Ethan is a few steps away, following this with pleasant interest. "So it's fixed?"

"The camera's fixed," Maya says. Something careful in the distinction.

I raise the camera, find an orange butterfly settled on a cluster of purple-blue flowers I don't know the name of, press the shutter.

Sharp image. Beautiful exposure, good light. Something's missing.

The butterfly has lifted off. What I have is a perfect photograph of where it was.

Maya watches me check the screen.

"You saw it settle and went for it." She's already turning back toward the path, back toward Ethan. "Wait until you're sure it's staying. Back up, give it room. Let it decide."

I hold the camera and look at the empty flower.

---

We spend another hour in the park, the three of us. Ethan carries most of it. I've seen him like this before: words coming fast, no gaps, one story folding into the next before the last one's finished landing. He does it when something matters too much to let go quiet. Like silence might let something in he's not ready for. She meets it well. Asks the right questions, laughs at the right moments, lets him find his pace.

I'm mostly to the side of things. Taking shots. Still missing butterflies. The autofocus is working now. The shots are sharp. Just empty. I catch Maya glancing at me a few times. Brief, not pointed. The kind of look that doesn't ask anything back. I notice every one of them.

The afternoon moves. Ethan talks and she listens; she talks and he leans in.

Her voice changes when she's talking about something she actually cares about. The register shifts, something easier in it. Once she laughed at something that wasn't quite funny and caught herself. Didn't cover it. Just let it be what it was.

I'm not sure when I started cataloguing this. I didn't decide to.

---

Maya's flat is the kind of place that makes you want to slow down. Photographs on every wall. Not decorative, actual work. A mountainside going grey in fog, the summit already gone. Whatever she went up there to photograph, she stayed for this instead. A child watching rain on a window from the inside. The glass fogged slightly where she'd leaned close. A market stall being folded down in fading light, seller's back turned. Unbothered by being seen.

Ethan finds her bookshelf and starts asking questions. He does this: finds the nearest object and begins, and there's nothing calculated about it, it's just how he is: genuinely curious, genuinely warm, the kind of person rooms rearrange themselves around. She hasn't looked my way since we walked in. I notice.

I'm standing in front of a photograph of an empty junction in early morning. Almost nobody in it. A stopped bike near the left edge, owner nowhere. The feeling in it is someone who waited for the street to almost empty. Who knew what they were waiting for.

"That one took three mornings," Maya says, close behind me.

I didn't hear her cross the room. "Why three?"

"Last February. I had to be there before the light moved on. Fourth morning it would've been gone."

The season. Not just the hour.

"You went quiet."

"The photograph." Not entirely a lie.

She looks at it, then back at me. "I have butterflies on the card from last spring. What it looks like when you don't go toward them."

---

Ethan excuses himself to find the bathroom.

We go to the kitchen table with the camera between us. She pulls the card, puts it in her laptop, opens the folder. A series of butterfly shots: each one arrested at the instant of stillness, wings fully open, every detail resolved. She clicks through them slowly.

"I waited for each of these," she says. "I'd find one that was feeding, wait for it to settle, then back up, get into position, and wait until I was certain before I touched the shutter."

"I keep going toward them."

"I know. I watched you." She's looking at the screen, not at me. "The butterfly reads the movement. It doesn't trust it."

She leans forward to scroll, and her arm comes to rest against mine on the table. The length of a forearm. Not a brush — contact, easy and unannounced, like something that hasn't decided yet if it means anything.

I don't move my arm.

She doesn't move hers.

She keeps her eyes on the screen. "Toward the end you stopped moving toward them. Another minute and you'd have had one."

"I didn't think you were paying attention."

She glances sideways. Holds it a moment. "I notice things." Then back to the screen.

My shoulder is almost touching hers now.

"Luke."

Just my name. Nothing else.

The room is very still. I can hear the clock on the wall. From down the hall, the sound of a tap running.

Ethan.

Who has written to her for four years. Who has never, in all the time I've known him, which is his entire life, looked at someone the way he looked at her before she'd said a word. Something loosened in him when he laughed beside her in the park. I didn't know it was tight.

I move my arm off the table. A small thing. Enough.

She reads it immediately. No flicker of hurt — she just tilts back slightly in her chair and wraps both hands around her mug. She's further away than she was.

"The camera was the easy part," she says. Her voice is entirely level. "The ones worth getting," she says. "You can come back for them."

The tap cuts off. Footsteps.

Ethan comes back into the room already talking, something about a book on her shelf. Maya turns to face him and she's already smiling, genuinely. Whatever just happened, she doesn't show it.

---

She makes pasta. Nothing complicated. Ethan talks and we let him. This is his afternoon. Four years of letters finally in the same room. Maya holds her side of it well. She has stories, and the way she tells them is specific in the right places, slightly self-deprecating, never showy. A missed train in Prague that turned into three days she didn't plan. A photograph she drove four hours to take and got wrong, and drove back the following week to get right.

Ethan is watching her with something uncomplicated in his face.

She finds me across the table, in the gaps. Not in any way he'd notice. A pause held a beat longer than it needed to be, before she glances back.

I keep still. It's harder than it looks.

---

Late evening. She walks us to the door.

Ethan hugs her. She holds it a beat longer than he expects.

"You're exactly how I thought," she says.

He pulls back enough to look at her. "Better or worse?"

"Just exactly."

He laughs. "Same time next week?"

"Don't make it another four years."

He steps outside.

She looks at me.

"I'm glad you came," she says. "Both of you."

The porch light catches the left side of her face and leaves the rest in shadow.

"Come back," she says. "When you're ready."

The thing between Ethan and Maya was always going to be real. So is this. She said it. I just haven't.

"I think I will," I say.

She nods. Holds the door. Watches me go.
